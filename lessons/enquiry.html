Section 01 · Theory

What is an enquiry?

An enquiry (US: inquiry) is a business message — letter or email — in which a potential buyer asks a supplier for information before buying: details about products, a catalogue, a price list, a quotation, discounts, delivery and payment conditions.

In short: you want to buy something, but first you need information. So you write to the supplier and ask. The supplier answers with an offer or a quotation (a document with prices). Nothing is bought yet — you are only asking.

Enquiries are usually the first contact between two companies. They create no legal obligation: the buyer is not committed to buy, and the supplier is not committed to sell. A binding contract is created only later, with the order and its acceptance. A good enquiry is specific: it says exactly what information is needed, so the supplier can answer quickly and completely.

In legal terms an enquiry is a mere invitation to treat: it invites the other party to make an offer. The supplier's reply — the quotation — is normally an offer; when the buyer places an order referring to that offer, a contract is formed. Well-written enquiries often signal serious purchasing intentions ("if your prices are competitive, we are prepared to place regular orders") to encourage keen conditions. In export trade, enquiries typically specify Incoterms, ports, and payment instruments such as the letter of credit.

The business chain — click a step

⚖ GOLDEN RULE An enquiry only asks. It does not oblige anyone to buy or sell. An enquiry asks for information; an order commits you to buy. Never mix the two: the enquiry creates no obligation, the order does. Enquiry = invitation to treat · Quotation = offer · Order = acceptance → contract. Keep the legal sequence clear: only the order/acceptance stage creates binding obligations.

PART ①

Header & subject line

The subject says what the email is about. Keep it short and clear.Include the topic and, if possible, a product reference: it helps the supplier route your mail and answer faster.Formal letters carry sender's address, date and inside address. In emails, a precise subject line measurably raises the response rate.

PART ②

Salutation

Use Dear Sir or Madam if you don't know the name.Dear Sir or Madam, (unknown name) or Dear Mr Smith / Dear Ms Harper, (known name). Never "Hi!" in a first business contact.When the contact person is known, use the surname. Note the comma after the salutation in British style.

PART ③

Opening — why you write

Say immediately why you are writing.Say where you found the supplier (a fair, an advert, a recommendation): it gives context and flatters them a little.A formal opening often uses passive or reporting structures: Your company was recommended to us… / We understand that…

PART ④

Who you are

One short sentence about your company.One line of company profile: the supplier understands your size and may offer better conditions to a serious buyer.Hint at volumes or distribution reach: it positions you as a valuable customer before you even ask.

PART ⑤

The request(s) — the core

Ask for what you need: catalogue, prices, delivery times.Be specific: quantities, model references, bullet lists of questions. Vague enquiries get vague answers.In export enquiries: Incoterm + port, MOQs, discount scales, and the payment instrument you intend to use.

PART ⑥

The incentive advanced

Not needed at this level — but nice to know it exists.A conditional promise of future business: If your prices are competitive… It motivates a keen offer.Classic formula: "If your prices are competitive, we are prepared to place substantial/regular orders" — plus agency or exclusivity hints where relevant.

PART ⑦

Expecting a reply

A fixed, friendly sentence before goodbye.We look forward to + -ing is the standard business closing. Note: hearing, not hear.Variants: We look forward to your early reply · Your prompt answer would be appreciated.

PART ⑧

Sign-off

No name at the start → Yours faithfully.Match it with the salutation (see the rule below) and add your position and company.In formal correspondence this pairing is still noticed — getting it wrong signals carelessness.

RULE · NO NAME
Dear Sir or Madam → Yours faithfully

You don't know who will read it.

RULE · WITH NAME
Dear Ms Harper → Yours sincerely

Trick: Sincerely ↔ Somebody you know.

Section 06 · Quality control

Before you press send

DO

  • Be specific: quantities, models, references.
  • Use bullet lists for multiple requests.
  • Use polite modals: could, would, may.
  • Say where you found the supplier.
  • Include full contact details and your position.
  • Write a precise subject line.

DON'T

  • Use slang, emojis or "Hi!" in a first contact.
  • Write vague requests like "send me everything".
  • Sound demanding: "Answer immediately!".
  • Confuse an enquiry with an order.
  • Forget the salutation or the sign-off.
  • Mix up faithfully and sincerely.
